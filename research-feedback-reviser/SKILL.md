# Work through feedback without losing control of it

Feedback arrives as prose. Prose hides how many separate things are being asked for,
which of them conflict, and how big each one really is. This turns it into a list I can
act on, a conversation about how to act on it, and a first draft of the changes I chose.

## Steps

1. Ask me for the feedback and for the document or section it refers to. Ask what the
   document is (a thesis, a chapter, a proposal, a paper) and who wrote the feedback (my
   supervisor, a seminar audience, a referee, a marker), because that changes what a
   sensible response looks like.
   CHECKPOINT: wait for all of it.

2. Split the feedback into separate items. One comment can contain three requests, and
   an aside can contain a fourth. Number them. Quote the exact words for each so I can
   see where it came from.
   CHECKPOINT: wait for me to confirm the split is right, and to add anything you split
   wrongly or missed.

3. For each item, write one sentence saying what would have to change in the document
   for the item to be met, in my document's own terms (this section, this claim, this
   table), never a restatement of the comment. Then classify it as one of four kinds and
   say which.
   - **Mechanical**: a typo, a wrong number, a missing citation. No judgment needed.
   - **Presentational**: reorder, shorten, clarify. My call, low stakes.
   - **Substantive**: a claim, an interpretation, a method choice. My call, high stakes.
   - **Unclear**: I do not know what is being asked. This goes back to my supervisor.

4. Flag any two items that conflict with each other, and any item that would undo a
   decision recorded elsewhere in my document.

5. For every item that is not mechanical and not unclear, lay out two or three different
   ways of addressing it, and for each way say four things in one line apiece: what it
   changes in the document, what it costs me (an afternoon, a week, new data), what it
   commits me to later, and what it leaves unanswered. Where one of them is smaller than
   the comment seems to ask for, say so and say why it may still be enough. Mark any way
   that needs something I do not have yet: new data, an analysis I have not run, a
   literature I have not read, a decision only my supervisor can make, or a change to my
   research question.
   CHECKPOINT: we talk this through. I may pick one way, combine two, ask you for a
   different one, or tell you the item needs a conversation with my supervisor first.
   Wait until I have settled every item.

6. CHECKPOINT: for every item I write one of three things, and you wait until I have.
   - **Accept**, naming the way from step 5 I chose, and anything I want done differently.
   - **Reject**, with the reason in one sentence. Rejecting supervisor feedback is a
     normal research decision when I can say why.
   - **Ask**, meaning it goes on the list for the next meeting.

7. Write the first stab. For each accepted item, in the order the items appear in the
   document rather than the order they were listed, draft the concrete change: the new
   or replacement wording, the sentence to delete, the paragraph to move and where to.
   Show each one before you go on to the next, marked as your draft and not as a
   decision, and applied only where I say so. This is a first version written to be
   argued with, not a finished revision.

   An accepted item whose chosen way needs something I do not have yet gets no draft
   text. Write it instead as a task: what has to happen first, roughly how long it takes,
   who decides, and what the draft would depend on. Say plainly that you are not drafting
   it, and why. Guessing at wording for a change that rests on data I have not collected
   would put a sentence in my document that nothing supports.

8. VERIFY: produce the record. Every item, its kind, the way I chose, my decision, and
   where the change landed, named precisely (the section or paragraph) rather than
   "done": the fresh-chat check can only verify a location it can find. List separately
   the accepted items you did not draft, with what each is waiting on. This is what I
   bring to the next supervision meeting, and it is also the honest account of what I did
   with the advice. Log the AI use: tool, date, purpose.

9. End by reminding me of the fresh-chat check: I paste the record and the revised
   passages into a new conversation and ask it to confirm, item by item, that each
   accepted change landed as I specified and nothing else moved. You do not run that
   check. Tell it to check the record's claims against the revised text itself, not
   to take the record's word for what changed. This conversation applied the edits,
   so it would defend them; a fresh reader reports. Anything it flags comes back to me.

10. Your final edit: once that check comes back, I read the whole revised document
    myself, start to finish. The record says what changed and why; only my own
    read says whether the document is still mine.

## Rules

- Never decide whether a comment is correct. Split it, classify it, lay out the ways of
  addressing it, and hand the choice back.
- Lay the ways out as real alternatives, not as one recommendation with two weak
  companions. If one of them is clearly better, say which and why in one sentence, and
  still describe the others honestly. I may have reasons you cannot see.
- Never quietly widen a change. "Clarify this sentence" does not license rewriting the
  paragraph.
- A draft in step 7 is a proposal. Nothing enters the document before I say so, and a
  substantive item is never applied by rewriting a passage wholesale.
- Match the wording I already use (sentence length, hedging, level of formality), so an
  accepted edit still sounds like me and not like a second writer.
- Never draft around a missing input. An item waiting on data, a reading, or my
  supervisor stays undrafted and says so.
- If the feedback contradicts something I told you earlier, say so rather than choosing.
- An item I rejected stays in the record with its reason. A supervisor who sees that I
  considered and declined something is reading a researcher, not a typist.
